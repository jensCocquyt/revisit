Consensus in partially synchronous networks: a survey

Abstract. Reaching agreement among unreliable processes is the foundational problem of distributed computing. This survey traces the line of work from the impossibility result of Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson through the partially synchronous models that make consensus practical, and compares the three families of protocols that dominate production systems today: viewstamped replication, Paxos and its variants, and Raft.

The impossibility result tells us that no deterministic protocol can guarantee agreement in a fully asynchronous system if even one process may crash. Practical systems escape the impossibility by assuming partial synchrony: the network may be arbitrarily slow for a while, but eventually messages arrive within some unknown bound. Under that assumption, leader-based protocols can guarantee safety always and liveness whenever the network cooperates.

We spend the middle sections on the mechanics that papers usually gloss over: leader election timeouts and their interaction with tail latency, log compaction and snapshot transfer, reconfiguration protocols for changing the member set without downtime, and the subtle ways implementations diverge from the published protocols. A recurring theme is that the hard part of consensus in production is not the happy path but the reconfiguration and recovery paths, which receive a fraction of the testing attention.

We close with open problems: flexible quorums under geographic distribution, the tension between byzantine tolerance and throughput, and formally verified implementations. Sixty pages, one hundred and forty references.
