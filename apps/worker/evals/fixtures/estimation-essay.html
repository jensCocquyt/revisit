Your estimates are fine, your intervals are lies

Every engineering organization I have worked with believes it has an estimation problem. Almost none of them do. The median estimate is usually within spitting distance of reality; what is broken is the interval around it — the pretense that a task estimated at five days will land between four and six, when the honest distribution has a long fat tail stretching to five weeks.

The tail is not noise, it is structure. Tasks blow up for a small number of recurring reasons: an undocumented dependency on another team, a data migration that cannot be rehearsed, an abstraction that turns out to be load-bearing for behavior nobody specified. None of these appear in the estimate because estimates are made about the work we can see, and blowups come from the work we cannot.

The fix is not better estimation, it is planning that survives bad estimates. Cut scope by default when the tail shows up, rather than extending deadlines; sequence the frightening tasks first, when the calendar still has slack; and treat any task that touches two teams as three tasks, because coordination is work even when no code is.

The deeper point is cultural. An organization that punishes honest intervals gets dishonest ones, and then builds roadmaps on them. The teams that ship predictably are not the ones that estimate best — they are the ones where saying "this could take five weeks" is safe, and where the plan changes when the evidence does.
