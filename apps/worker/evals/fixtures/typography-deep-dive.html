Why your interface text looks wrong: a working developer's typography

You can feel bad typography before you can see it. An interface reads as cheap or tiring and nobody on the team can say why, because the causes hide below the threshold of casual inspection: line lengths a third too long, line height inherited from a reset instead of chosen, and four slightly different grays performing the role of hierarchy. This piece is the checklist I wish someone had handed me a decade ago.

Start with measure — the length of a line of text. Between 45 and 75 characters is the comfortable band for body copy; interfaces routinely ship 110 because containers are sized to layouts, not to reading. Cap prose containers at around 65ch and half of your perceived polish problems evaporate before lunch.

Line height is not a constant, it is a function of measure and size: short lines and large text want tight spacing, long lines and small text want loose. Body copy usually lands between 1.4 and 1.6; headings almost always want less than you think, often 1.1. The reset default of 1.5 everywhere is why your headings feel like they are floating away from themselves.

Hierarchy needs at most three levels of visual distinction, built from two sizes and two weights, not from six grays. If everything is slightly different, nothing is different; readers parse hierarchy from consistent contrast, and each additional in-between shade taxes that parsing. Pick your levels, name them as design tokens, and delete every color that is not one of them.

None of this requires taste. It requires measuring what you shipped against numbers that have been stable since the era of metal type, and the discipline to fix the boring things first.
